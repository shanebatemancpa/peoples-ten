Theory of Change · Foundational Document
The People's Ten
How sustained civic accountability emerges from small groups
01 · The Problem
American civic engagement is broken — and the technology that broke it has been treating the symptoms.
For two decades, every measurable indicator of civic health has fallen. Trust in government sits at 8%. Approval at 12%. Voter turnout has stagnated at one of the lowest rates in the developed world. Polarization has hardened into identity rather than ideology. Americans no longer disagree with one another — they no longer recognize one another.
The platforms designed to keep us informed have learned that outrage drives engagement and engagement drives revenue. They have no economic incentive to fix what they have built. They optimize for time-on-platform, which means optimizing for fear and contempt. The country fragmenting into algorithmic tribes is not a side effect of social media. It is its central product.
89,000,000
Eligible Americans who did not vote in 2024
They are not apathetic. They are not lost. They are unconvinced their participation matters — and twenty years of civic engagement technology has failed to change their minds.
The Core Problem
Existing civic engagement tools operate top-down — broadcasting messages to passive audiences. They lack the social architecture required to create durable behavior change at scale. People do not change their civic behavior because of a notification. They change it because the people sitting next to them do.
02 · The Insight
Behavior changes when neighbors change — not when platforms broadcast.
Network science has produced a clear answer to how durable behavior change spreads through human communities. It is called complex contagion: behaviors propagate through small groups when individuals receive multiple, reinforcing signals from people they trust. They do not spread virally from a single source. They spread laterally through tight clusters.
Damon Centola's research at the University of Pennsylvania has demonstrated this experimentally. So has Marshall Ganz's organizing methodology at Harvard. So has every successful social movement in American history. The civil rights movement was not won by mass media. It was won by congregations, classrooms, dinner tables, barber shops, and bus drivers who knew each other.
Starlings provide the most elegant model in nature. A flock of ten thousand birds executes coordinated turns at the speed of thought. There is no leader. There is no central command. Each bird watches only its six or seven nearest neighbors, applies three local rules, and the flock as a whole becomes a single intelligent organism. This is murmuration.
Human civic behavior can be engineered to follow the same pattern. Not commanded — engineered. The conditions can be designed. The architecture can be built. The technology now exists to do this at scale, and for the first time in modern history, the cost is low enough that it can be done outside of corporate or partisan control.
The Insight
If we engineer the right conditions in groups of six to eight existing relationships — book clubs, parent groups, church small groups, neighbors, hunting buddies, running clubs — we can create civic murmuration. The technology that broke us apart can put us back together, six neighbors at a time.
03 · The Chain of Change
From ten simple rules to a country that holds itself accountable.
The theory of change rests on a clear chain. Each link is necessary. None is sufficient alone.
Step 01
Ten Simple Rules
A non-partisan, fixed set of standards every public officeholder should commit to in word and deed.
→
Step 02
Small Group Architecture
Existing groups of 6-8 adopt the Ten as a shared reference point. Trust already exists. We add purpose.
→
Step 03
Local Visibility
AI surfaces real-time accountability data — votes, statements, actions — at the neighborhood scale.
Step 04
Conditional Voting
Members make their vote conditional on candidate adherence to the Ten. Across all parties. No exceptions.
→
Step 05
Murmuration
The flock effect emerges. Groups feel connected to a national movement without losing local trust.
→
Step 06
Accountability Becomes Norm
Officeholders know they will be measured. Behavior changes upstream. The 89 million become decisive.
Each step in the chain is grounded in established research. The novelty is the combination — and the application of modern AI infrastructure to make the local visibility step feasible for the first time.
04 · The If-Then-Because
The logic stated plainly.
If
we provide existing groups of 6-8 trusted neighbors with shared accountability principles, low-friction tools to apply them, and real-time local visibility into how their elected officials measure up,
Then
civic accountability behavior will propagate through these groups via complex contagion, sustained by mutual reinforcement and local social proof,
Because
behavior change at scale has been demonstrated empirically to require trusted small-group networks, shared identity signals, and tangible action — and modern AI makes the third element feasible for the first time at the neighborhood scale.
Result
Officeholders behave knowing they are watched. Voters who had checked out re-engage because their participation now visibly matters. Civic engagement compounds across election cycles rather than collapsing after each one.

06 · Underlying Assumptions
What must be true for this to work.
Honesty about assumptions is what separates a theory of change from a marketing plan. Every assumption below must be tested before scale. If any of them fails, the model fails.
Assumption 01
Complex contagion works for civic behavior, not just consumer behavior or health behavior.
Tested by: Centola conversation. Pilot data. Existing research on civic activist development (Han).
Assumption 02
Existing small groups will adopt civic action without it disrupting their primary purpose.
Tested by: Pilot in book clubs, church small groups, parent networks. Friction measurement.
Assumption 03
A non-partisan framework can survive contact with a hyper-partisan environment without being captured.
Tested by: Mason conversation. Structural governance design. Bipartisan board from day one.
Assumption 04
AI-driven local accountability data can be produced reliably and inexpensively at neighborhood scale.
Tested by: Conor / data architecture review. Cost modeling. Accuracy benchmarking.
Assumption 05
The 89 million who didn't vote will respond to a frame other than partisan persuasion.
Tested by: Pilot turnout data. Pre/post engagement surveys. Comparison to control communities.
Assumption 06
Patient capital and governance can be assembled to sustain a 10-20 year build.
Tested by: Foundation conversations. 501(c)(3) / (c)(4) structure design. Founder succession plan.
07 · Risks & Mitigations
What could kill this — and how we respond.
Risk 01
Partisan capture. One side claims the Ten as a mascot, the other rejects it. Mitigated by structural bipartisan governance and a board veto on any politicized communication.
Risk 02
Platform decay. Initial enthusiasm wanes after first election cycle. Mitigated by predictable annual rhythm, off-year accountability programming, and leadership development from day one.
Risk 03
Information weaponization. Bad actors flood the platform with manufactured accountability data. Mitigated by source verification standards, citation requirements, and peer-review of the Ten's measurement criteria.
Risk 04
Scale collapse. Local trust dissolves as the platform grows national. Mitigated by architectural commitment to small-group primacy — the local layer is never compromised for national reach.
Risk 05
Founder dependence. Project dies if founder leaves. Mitigated by institutional governance, distributed leadership, and a documented handoff process from year one.
08 · Why Now
Three forces converge for the first time in modern civic history.
The crisis is acute. Trust in institutions is at historic lows. The 2024 election demonstrated that 89 million eligible Americans see no reason to participate. Continuing the current civic engagement playbook is choosing to fail in slow motion.
The technology is ready. AI infrastructure that can read every public statement, track every vote, and surface that information at the neighborhood scale was prohibitively expensive eighteen months ago. It is now feasible. This is the first generation of civic engagement tools that can actually deliver on hyper-local accountability at scale.
The research is mature. Centola's complex contagion. Ganz's snowflake organizing. Han's activist development. Mason's identity work. The behavioral science needed to design this correctly has been developed over the last twenty years. It has never been integrated into a single applied platform.
Why Now
The same twenty years that produced the polarization crisis also produced the research and the technology required to engineer our way out. We do not have to invent any of the underlying science. We have to assemble it correctly, fund it patiently, and trust the small groups to do what they have always done: take care of each other.
The Ask
Help Us Validate, Then Help Us Build.
Before another line of code or another scene of video gets made, this theory of change needs to be tested by the people who have spent careers studying these dynamics. If they tell us it will not work, we listen. If they tell us it will, we build the institution that holds American civic life accountable for the next generation.

Either way, we owe the 89 million an honest answer.
